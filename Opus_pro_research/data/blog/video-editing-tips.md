## Hooks and the First 3 Seconds

The edit that matters most is the one viewers see before they decide to scroll. Get this wrong and nothing else in your timeline gets watched.

**1. Win the first 3 seconds or lose the view.** Viewers decide whether to keep watching almost instantly. Lead with your single most interesting moment — not a logo, not "hey guys," not a slow build.

**2. Open with a shock or unexpected statement.** In our hook analysis, "shock/surprise" openers were the single most common hook in high-performing clips. Say the thing that makes someone stop scrolling.

**3. Or open with a direct question.** Direct questions and intriguing promises were the next most common hook category. "Why does nobody talk about this?" pulls people in because they want the answer.

**4. Cut your intro to zero.** Branded intros appear in only about 1% of clips for a reason — on social, they're watch-time poison. Save the branding for the outro.

**5. Front-load the payoff, then explain.** Show the result first ("here's the $12 edit that looks like $1,200"), then walk back through how you got there.

**6. Match the hook to the first frame.** Whatever promise your opening frame makes, the first line of audio should confirm it within a second.

**7. Re-cut your hook three different ways.** Export three versions of the opening and post them on different days. Hooks are the highest-leverage A/B test you can run.

## Captions and On-Screen Text

If there is one thing the data screams, it's this: captions are no longer optional.

**8. Always add captions.** They appear in **80.2% of the clips we analyzed** — the single most-used editing feature by a wide margin. Most social video is watched on mute.

**9. Animate your captions. 78.6% of clips use animated (word-by-word or pop-on) captions**, not static blocks. Animation guides the eye and measurably holds attention.

**10. Keep captions to one or two words per beat.** Punchy, fast captions feel modern and match how people actually read on a phone.

**11. Put captions in the lower third — but not the very bottom.** Platform UI (usernames, buttons) covers the bottom \~15% of the frame. Keep text safely above it.

**12. Use high-contrast styling.** White text with a dark outline or a subtle background box reads on any footage. Test it against your brightest and busiest shots.

**13. Skip ALL CAPS for full sentences.** Mixed case is easier to read and feels less like shouting. Reserve caps for one or two emphasis words.

**14. Sync emphasis to your voice.** Pop a word to a brighter color or bold exactly when you stress it out loud. It reinforces the point twice.

**15. Proofread auto-captions for homophones and names.** AI captions are fast and accurate, but they'll still write "there" for "their" and butcher proper nouns. A 30-second pass fixes it.

**16. Add a text overlay for your one key point.** Only \~4.5% of clips use dedicated text overlays — which means a single, well-placed on-screen line makes you stand out.

**17. Translate captions to reach a second audience.** Duplicating a caption track in another language is one of the cheapest ways to expand reach.

## Pacing, Cuts, and Trimming

After captions, pacing is the biggest lever you have. Tight videos get watched to the end; baggy ones get scrolled.

**18. Be ruthless with dead air.** Cut pauses, filler words ("um," "uh," "like"), and false starts. If a moment doesn't add information or energy, it goes.

**19. Use silence removal for the first pass.** AI silence removers strip gaps automatically and can cut editing time dramatically — see our roundup of the [best AI silence removers](/blog/best-ai-silence-removers).

**20. Make a cut every few seconds.** Even a subtle reframe or zoom resets attention. Static talking-head shots are where viewers drop off.

**21. Master the jump cut.** It's the backbone of modern short-form. [Auto jump-cut editors](/blog/auto-jump-cut-editors) can detect and tighten these for you.

**22. Use J-cuts and L-cuts to smooth transitions.** Let the next clip's audio start before its video (J-cut), or let the current audio linger over the next shot (L-cut). It makes edits feel invisible.

**23. Cut on motion.** Hiding a cut inside a movement — a hand gesture, a head turn — makes it far less jarring than cutting on a still frame.

**24. Vary your shot length.** A run of same-length clips feels robotic. Mix quick beats with the occasional longer hold to control rhythm.

**25. End a beat early, not late.** Leave on the upswing. The instinct to let a moment "breathe" usually just adds drag.

## B-Roll and Visual Variety

Here's a genuine opportunity hiding in the data.

**26. Add more B-roll than you think you need.** Only **6.0% of clips use B-roll** — making it one of the most underused techniques. That's exactly why it differentiates you.

**27. Cover your talking head with cutaways.** Every time you mention a thing, show the thing. B-roll over narration keeps eyes on the screen during explanation-heavy stretches.

**28. Use B-roll to hide your cuts.** A cutaway is the cleanest way to splice two takes together without a visible jump.

**29. Keep a personal B-roll library.** Shoot generic establishing and texture shots whenever you film. Reusable footage saves hours later.

**30. Layer simple motion on stills.** A slow push-in or pan (the "Ken Burns" move) turns a static image into something that holds attention.

## Audio: The Half Everyone Ignores

Audiences forgive rough video. They will not forgive bad audio.

**31. Treat audio as half the edit.** Clean sound is the fastest way to make amateur footage feel professional.

**32. Remove noise, hum, and clicks first.** Run denoise before you do anything else — you can't un-hear a buzzing room tone.

**33. Record 20–30 seconds of room silence.** That "nothing" is invaluable for filling gaps in the edit without a jarring cut to dead air.

**34. Mix music under, not over.** Duck music well beneath dialogue. If music competes with your voice, your voice loses.

**35. Use music to set pace, then get out of the way.** A driving track under a montage works; the same track under a key explanation is a distraction. (Music appears in under 2% of clips — use it deliberately, not by default.)

**36. Cut to the beat in montages.** Aligning your cuts to the music's rhythm makes even simple footage feel intentional.

**37. Normalize loudness for the platform.** Wildly different clip volumes send viewers reaching for the volume slider — and then the scroll.

## Structure: Outros, CTAs, and Retention

Most creators stop thinking here — and lose the conversion.

**38. End with a real outro.** Outros appear in **4.6% of clips** — more than intros — because they're where you convert a viewer into a follower. Don't just trail off.

**39. Make your CTA specific.** "Follow for part two" beats "like and subscribe." Tell people exactly what they get and what to do.

**40. Loop the ending back to the start.** On short-form, an ending that flows into the opening line buys you a second play — and replays boost ranking.

**41. Tease the next video.** A one-line hook for what's coming converts a single view into a subscriber.

**42. Put your strongest 5 seconds where the dropoff is.** Check your retention graph, find the cliff, and move something compelling there.

## Platform-Specific Editing

Different feeds reward different edits. Match the platform or pay for it in reach.

**43. TikTok: fast, native, sound-on culture.** Quick cuts, trend audio, and bold captions win. See our data-backed [TikTok editing guide](/research/video-editing-guide-tiktok).

**44. YouTube (and Shorts): hooks plus retention structure.** Longer attention spans reward clear structure and chapters. Our [YouTube editing guide](/research/video-editing-guide-youtube) breaks down what top channels do.

**45. Instagram: polished and aesthetic-first.** Clean text, cohesive color, and on-brand styling matter more here. Details in the [Instagram editing guide](/research/video-editing-guide-instagram).

**46. Short-form everywhere: vertical, captioned, front-loaded.** The universal rules for 9:16 are in our [short-form editing guide](/research/video-editing-guide-short-form).

**47. Export at the platform's native spec.** 9:16 vertical for TikTok, Reels, and Shorts; 16:9 for standard YouTube. Letterboxed or wrong-ratio video reads as reposted spam.
