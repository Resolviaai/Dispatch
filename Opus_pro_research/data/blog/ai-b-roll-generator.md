# AI B-Roll Generator: How to Add B-Roll to Videos Automatically

B-roll is the footage that plays over your voice — the supporting shots, clips, and visuals that cut away from your face to illustrate what you're saying. It's the single biggest thing separating a flat talking-head clip from something that looks produced. The catch has always been the work: finding relevant footage, cutting it in, and timing it to your words is slow, which is why most creators skip it. An **AI B-roll generator** removes that work — it reads what you're saying and adds the right supporting footage automatically.

This guide covers what B-roll is, how much to use, the types you can pull from, how it's added manually versus automatically, what an AI B-roll generator actually does under the hood, how to use it well, B-roll ideas by content type, and how our [AI producer](/blog/what-is-an-ai-producer) adds it for you as part of a full edit.

## What is B-roll?

Every video has two layers. **A-roll** is your primary footage — you, talking to camera. **B-roll** is everything that cuts away from it: a product shot, a screen recording, stock footage of a city, an animated chart, a photo, a quick demo. When you say "our users doubled their output" and the screen briefly shows a rising graph, that graph is B-roll. It illustrates the point, adds visual variety, and covers the moment so the viewer never gets bored of a single static frame. The term comes from film, where the "A" reel held the main shots and the "B" reel held the cutaways an editor could drop in — the idea has just moved to short-form, where it matters more than ever because attention is measured in seconds.

## Why B-roll matters

- **Retention.** Cutting to a relevant visual resets the viewer's attention. A single unbroken talking-head shot is one of the fastest ways to lose the scroll, and retention is the metric every platform's algorithm rewards most.
- **Clarity.** Showing the thing you're describing is more effective than describing it. B-roll does the explaining your words can't — a product in use, a before-and-after, a number on screen.
- **Production value.** B-roll is the cheapest way to make a phone recording look like it had a crew behind it. Two or three good cutaways change the whole perceived quality of a clip.
- **Pacing.** Cutaways hide edits and control rhythm, so a tightened take feels intentional rather than choppy. They give you somewhere to cut to when you remove filler.

## How much B-roll should a video have?

There's no fixed ratio, but a useful rule for talking-head short-form is that B-roll should *punctuate*, not dominate. Cut away on the specific moments that benefit — a claim, a number, a product, a process — and return to your face in between, because the face-to-camera moments are what build trust. A common failure mode is covering the entire clip in stock footage; at that point you've made a stock-footage montage with a voiceover, not a creator video. As a rough guide, a sixty-second talking-head clip might use three to six short cutaways of two to four seconds each. Longer educational or tutorial content leans more heavily on B-roll (screen recordings, diagrams) because there's more to show.

## Types of B-roll

- **Stock footage.** Licensed clips from libraries — the traditional source, huge selection, but the slowest to search and easy to make look generic.
- **Original footage.** Your own clips: the product, the workspace, the process, the result. The most authentic and on-brand option.
- **Screen recordings.** Essential for software, tutorials, data, and demos — often the single most useful B-roll for founders and educators.
- **Motion graphics and text.** Animated words, numbers, arrows, and shapes that emphasize a point or make a stat land.
- **AI-generated clips.** Short generated visuals for concepts you can't easily film — abstract ideas, impossible shots, stylized scenes.
- **Photos and images.** A single well-timed image — a screenshot, a chart, a headline — is often all a moment needs, and it's the fastest B-roll of all.

## How to add B-roll manually (the slow way)

Done by hand, B-roll is a multi-step chore. You go through your transcript and mark the phrases that need illustrating. You search stock libraries or your own files for a clip that fits each one. You download the clips, import them, cut them into the timeline, trim them to length, and nudge each so it lands on the exact word. Then you adjust levels so any clip audio doesn't fight your voice, and you re-watch to make sure nothing drags. For a single sixty-second video that's easily thirty to sixty minutes of finicky work — which is precisely why so many otherwise-good talking-head videos never get any B-roll at all. The intent is there; the time isn't.

## AI-generated B-roll vs. stock B-roll

There are two ways an AI tool can supply B-roll, and they suit different needs. **Sourced (stock) B-roll** pulls real, licensed footage — ideal when you want something that clearly looks real (a real city, a real office, real hands typing). **Generated B-roll** creates a visual from scratch — useful for abstract concepts, stylized looks, or shots that would be impossible or expensive to film. In practice the strongest workflow uses both: real footage or your own clips for anything concrete, and generated visuals for the conceptual moments. The key is relevance either way — B-roll that doesn't match the words is worse than none, because it reads as filler and pulls attention away from your point.

## How to use B-roll well

- **Put it on the words that need it.** Illustrate the specific noun, number, or moment — not every sentence.
- **Keep it short.** Two to four seconds is usually plenty. Long cutaways lose the thread and make viewers forget who's talking.
- **Don't hide your face.** In a talking-head video the trust comes from seeing you; B-roll should punctuate, not bury.
- **Match the tone.** The clip's energy, color, and pace should fit the moment, or it reads as random stock dropped in to fill space.
- **Time it to the word.** B-roll that lands a beat late feels broken. Landing the cut on the exact word it illustrates is what makes it feel produced.
- **Don't overdo it.** Wall-to-wall B-roll is as bad as none — it turns your video into someone else's footage.

## B-roll ideas by content type

- **Educational / how-to.** Screen recordings, diagrams, animated steps, and text callouts that show each stage as you explain it.
- **Product / SaaS.** Screen captures of the product, UI close-ups, before-and-after results, and quick demos on the feature you're naming.
- **Founder / thought leadership.** Charts and numbers when you cite data, headlines or logos for social proof, and workspace or team shots for texture.
- **Podcast clips.** Guest names and titles as lower-thirds, topic-relevant cutaways on key points, and quote text on the sharpest lines.

## Common B-roll mistakes

- **Generic stock that doesn't match.** A random office clip on an unrelated point reads as filler and cheapens the video.
- **Covering your whole face-to-camera moment.** You lose the connection that makes talking-head content work in the first place.
- **Mistimed cuts.** B-roll that lands a beat late feels broken; timing to the word is what sells it.
- **Too much.** If viewers forget there's a person presenting, you've over-cut.
- **Loud clip audio.** Forgetting to duck the audio on a sourced clip so it competes with your voice.
