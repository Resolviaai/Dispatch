# What Is Clipping? How Film, TV & Music IPs are Winning New Audiences in 2026

Clipping is how a 40-year-old album, a mid-season streaming show, and a brand new single all end up on the same For You page. It is now one of the most-used promotional channels in entertainment, and most people scrolling past it have no idea it is marketing at all.

This is a breakdown of what clipping actually is, why it works so well for entertainment IP specifically, how a campaign runs from brief to payout, who the major agencies are, and where the tooling fits.

## What is clipping?

Clipping is the practice of taking long-form content and redistributing it as short-form clips across hundreds or thousands of independent social accounts, with the people posting those clips paid based on the views they generate.

In practice, that means taking anything long, whether it is a music video, a live performance, a behind-the-scenes reel, or a sit-down interview, and cutting it into short pieces built for a feed. (Music Ally’s [explainer on clipping in music](https://musically.com/2025/11/21/what-is-clipping-music-tiktok-trend/) is a good deeper read.)

The important distinction is what clipping is *not*. It is not influencer marketing.

In a traditional creator campaign, a label pays a handful of curated influencers a flat fee to post about a release. In a clipping campaign, an agency provides approved footage to a large network of independent clippers, who each cut their own version and post it to their own account. Nobody gets a flat fee. Everyone gets paid per thousand views.

The difference shows up in how it looks. Clipping resembles the fan content that springs up on its own around a song or a cultural moment, not like a small, hand-picked group of creators posting on cue for a release. The result is high-volume, native-looking distribution rather than a few polished placements.

The tactic started in livestreaming. Creators like Adin Ross, Kai Cenat, Druski, and MrBeast built early clipper armies to pull highlights out of hours-long streams. Music adopted it next. Film and television are adopting it now.

## Why clipping works so well for entertainment IP

Entertainment is arguably the best-suited category for clipping of any industry, for a few structural reasons.

### The content already exists

This is the big one. A studio, label, or production company is sitting on an enormous archive: music videos, performance footage, press junkets, behind-the-scenes reels, episodes, trailers, interviews, awards show appearances. Clipping monetizes that archive without asking anyone to produce anything new.

For music in particular, this solves a real and expensive problem. Asking artists to produce fresh social content every day burns them out and strains the relationship with their teams. Clipping sidesteps that, since [most campaigns need nothing new from the artist](https://musically.com/2025/11/21/what-is-clipping-music-tiktok-trend/) to get started.

The same logic applies to a show that has already wrapped or a film that is already in the can. The asset is finished. Clipping just puts it in front of people.

### The For You page fragmented, and clipping targets the pieces

The old playbook of paying one huge creator for a single post has stopped working, because the For You page is now split into thousands of niches and no single post reaches all of them. [Music marketers interviewed by Music Ally](https://musically.com/2025/11/21/what-is-clipping-music-tiktok-trend/) describe clipping as the answer: a way to go after those niches one at a time.

Instead of one big placement, a campaign can push into anime edits, TV edits, concert edits, gym content, and film analysis simultaneously, testing which pocket of the internet responds hardest. That is much closer to how entertainment audiences actually cluster.

### The cost structure is not close

Clipping campaigns run on CPM, and the numbers are dramatically lower than paid media. [Industry estimates](https://musically.com/2025/11/21/what-is-clipping-music-tiktok-trend/) put a typical campaign at roughly $1,000 per million views, a small fraction of what the same impressions cost through paid social ads.

Published campaign results back this up. [The Clip Ship reports](https://www.theclipship.com/) ending CPMs of $0.16 on a Logic album rollout and $0.60 on a Netflix title.

### It reaches the audience that does not watch ads

Clips do not enter the ad system, so platforms treat them as regular organic posts and distribute them accordingly. For entertainment brands trying to reach viewers under 30, who skip advertising by default, that is the whole ballgame. It is also why clipping has become a go-to for catalog rediscovery: introducing a 1960s performance or a decade-old sync to an audience that was never going to encounter it otherwise.

### It is not just for new releases

Clipping is just as useful for bringing older catalog back into circulation: album anniversaries, holiday records, and sync moments when a track shows up in a new season of a show. The same is true for a streaming back catalog: a show’s fourth season dropping is a reason to re-clip seasons one through three.

*"Traditional marketing is running out of road. Billboards are stuck in one zip code, and people have tuned ads out. Clipping meets consumers where they're actually making the decision to buy: to stream a show, grab a ticket, whatever that next step looks like. There's a psychology behind it too. When the algorithm puts something in your feed, there's an implicit endorsement baked in: it thinks you'll like this, so you trust it more than an ad. And because clips keep living online long after spend stops, distributing at scale in the feeds doesn't just seed content during the release window. It keeps finding new audiences who didn't even know they were fans yet."*

**- Alec Schweitzer, founder and CEO, The Clip Ship**

## How clipping campaigns actually work

Most entertainment clipping runs through an agency rather than in-house, and most agency campaigns are hosted on Whop’s Content Rewards. Here is the full loop.

### 1. The agency sets up the campaign

The label, studio, or artist team hands over approved source material and agrees on the objective. The agency turns that into a campaign: a budget, a payout rate per thousand views, eligible platforms, and a set of rules.

Those rules do real work. They cover which hashtags to use, who to tag, minimum clip length, required on-screen text, and sometimes geographic targeting. Agencies typically supply a curated selection of footage rather than the raw archive, often somewhere between four and ten moments for clippers to choose from per campaign.

The campaign then goes live on a platform like Content Rewards, where it sits in a public marketplace alongside everything else currently recruiting.

### 2. Clippers find the campaign and join

Independent clippers browse the marketplace, filter by category and payout rate, and join the campaigns that fit what they are good at. There is no follower minimum and usually no application. Someone who runs a film-edit page can pick up a studio campaign the same day it launches.

This is why the agency model scales. The Clip Ship reports a network of 175,000+ creators. Clipping Culture reports 300,000+. A campaign can be in front of thousands of potential posters within hours of going live.

### 3. Clippers cut and post the content

Each clipper pulls the approved footage, finds a moment worth posting, edits it into a short, and publishes it to their own TikTok, Reels, or Shorts account. This is where tooling matters, and where most clippers now use AI editors like OpusClip to get from a two-hour source file to a batch of finished clips in minutes rather than an evening.

Volume is the whole game here. A campaign’s reach is the sum of every clipper’s output, so anything that raises output per clipper raises campaign performance.

### 4. Submission, verification, and payout

Clippers submit their post URLs back through the campaign. The agency reviews each submission against the guidelines, rejects anything non-compliant, and verifies the views that count. Payouts run on verified views up to a per-clip cap.

That verification step is what separates a real campaign from a vanity metric, and reputable agencies report results only on views they have verified. Turnaround from assets to live clips is fast across the board, typically one to two days.

**Film and television**

- **SERHANT, “Owning Manhattan” (Netflix).** The Clip Ship ran a campaign around the series that reached the Netflix Top 10, at a reported ending CPM of $0.60.
- **Super Mario Galaxy.** An animation and gaming IP campaign run across 15 creators at a $0.41 ending CPM.
- **Harry Styles on “Royal Court.”** [According to Variety](https://variety.com/2026/music/news/clipping-marketing-tool-took-over-music-industry-1236699705/), Whop hosted a clipping campaign around the appearance on Brittany Broski’s podcast, an example of talent-driven press being clipped as distribution rather than just coverage.

**Music**

- **bbno$.** Clipping Culture’s largest published case study: 2.01 billion verified views across 25,245 clips. An earlier phase of the work [reportedly generated](https://www.netinfluencer.com/college-dropout-builds-clipping-agency-driving-billions-of-views-for-brands-musicians/) 190 million views on a $9,000 budget.
- **Logic, “Paradise Records.”** The Clip Ship generated 125M+ views on the album rollout at a $0.16 CPM.
- **Drake and PartyNextDoor.** [According to Music Ally’s coverage](https://musically.com/2025/11/21/what-is-clipping-music-tiktok-trend/), their campaign for a recent joint album passed 100 million views.
- **John Summit, “Lights Go Out.”** [Variety reported](https://variety.com/2026/music/news/clipping-marketing-tool-took-over-music-industry-1236699705/) that a campaign with a $1,050 initial budget produced 32.4 million views and 1.4 million likes across 29 approved clips from 13 creators in eight days. The top clip alone did 5.3 million.
- **ian, “Magic Johnson.”** Widely cited as the first breakout case of an artist built substantially through clipping. He now has an XXL Freshman placement and 100M+ Spotify streams.
- **Others with public campaigns:** Yung Gravy (401M+ views), Lucki, RUSS, The Rolling Stones, Lady Gaga, Selena Gomez, Jonas Brothers, Tate McRae, Mumford & Sons, and Brandi Carlile.

The pattern across all of them: nobody in front of the camera had to do anything new.

## The takeaway

Clipping has moved from a livestreaming curiosity to a standard line item in entertainment marketing budgets, and the reason is not complicated. It monetizes footage that already exists, it reaches an audience that actively avoids advertising, it costs a fraction of paid media per impression, and it does not require the talent to do anything at all.

For rights holders sitting on a catalog, the question has quietly shifted from whether to clip to who runs it and how fast it can go live.
