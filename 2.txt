## New findings that could change the design

**1. The recording requirement has an important iOS limit.** iOS prohibits camera use while an app is in the background. Android can record through a foreground service, but it must be started while the app is visible and meet current camera and microphone service rules. So “start recording, lock the phone, and leave it running” cannot be a cross-platform promise. Keep the recording screen active, make the recording state clear, and test long sessions across real devices. Once recording stops, background upload is a separate, more tractable problem. [Apple’s camera interruption docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfoundation/avcapturesession/interruptionreason/videodevicenotavailableinbackground), [Android foreground-service rules](https://developer.android.com/develop/background-work/services/fgs/restrictions-bg-start?hl=en)

**2. ClippyMe is the closest newly surfaced end-to-end project.** It combines highlight selection, reframing, captions, browser editing, and publishing; its repo is MIT licensed and shows active development. Its README says it began as a fork of OpenShorts and credits ClipsAI, FrameShift, and VideoLingo for ideas it adopted. I’d study it as a **working product reference**, especially its flow and job-state handling, rather than assume it is a drop-in backend: it leans on services such as Gemini, Deepgram, and Zernio. [ClippyMe](https://github.com/fralapo/clippyme)

A licensing detail matters if this ever becomes a commercial product: ClippyMe’s MIT license does not settle the licenses of its dependencies. Audit the specific model, code, and distribution path before adopting that dependency. [Ultralytics licensing](https://www.ultralytics.com/license)

**3. There’s a useful CPU-only reframing baseline.** [VerticalMaker](https://github.com/RishikeshAluguvelli/VerticalMaker) offers fill, speaker-following, and stacked-speaker layouts using OpenCV and FFmpeg. Its speaker choice uses mouth movement plus audio energy, so it’s a practical cheap baseline for interviews and podcasts—not robust audiovisual speaker identification. For that harder case, [TalkNet](https://github.com/TaoRuijie/TalkNet_ASD) is a research implementation of active-speaker detection. Treat it as an optional accuracy upgrade that needs evaluation on your footage, not a required V1 dependency.

**4. ClipsAI and Lighthouse fill different parts of the highlight gap.** [ClipsAI](https://github.com/ClipsAI/clipsai) is an MIT-licensed Python library for transcript-based clip finding and reframing, especially for narrative audio such as interviews and speeches. It’s a reusable component to compare against your current clip-selection approach. [Lighthouse](https://github.com/line/lighthouse) is a reproducible library for video and audio moment retrieval and highlight detection. It’s more useful for evaluating or experimenting with visual highlight models than for plugging directly into a production workflow.

For a low-cost first version, I’d rank transcript moments first, then inspect sparse frames only around promising candidates. Full-video visual analysis is an expensive way to search for moments that often start with speech.

## Storage can stay temporary, but allow for the overlap

As a rough example, four hours of video at 8 Mbps is about **14 GB**. At 20 Mbps it is about **36 GB**. The phone’s actual bitrate varies with resolution, frame rate, and device. Fifty 30-second clips encoded at 8 Mbps would be about **1.5 GB** total; 60-second clips about **3 GB**.

The raw footage and finished clips will coexist briefly while the system processes and checks the outputs. A sensible flow is:

1. Keep the original until all chosen clips are rendered and checked.
2. Delete the original once those clips are safe.
3. Delete each clip only after the platform confirms it has finished processing the upload and the local publish record is updated.

Keep small job records—timestamps, clip decisions, upload IDs, and statuses—after deleting the media. They take little space and prevent a restart from silently losing track of what happened. Do not delete a clip just because the upload request returned; an ambiguous timeout needs checking before retrying.

## What I'd change from the earlier stack

- **Record in portrait.** A 16:9 source cropped to 9:16 uses only a 608×1080 slice of a 1080p frame, so it ends up soft. If you're the only person on camera and record vertically from the start, you can skip reframing and speaker detection. They only matter for multi-person footage.

## Making it look like normal clipping

The risk isn't detection. It's that templated output performs badly and can cost you reach or monetization. YouTube's policy targets mass-produced content using templates and reused or repetitive content with minimal value. Meta is demoting accounts that repost without meaningful enhancements. YouTube has said clips and reaction channels aren't the target, so clips of your own original footage are fine if they don't look factory-made. In practice:

- Don't post 50 clips in a burst. Spread them over weeks with randomized posting times.
- Use two or three caption styles, not one default word-by-word look.
- Write titles and hooks in your own voice, using examples you've actually written. Avoid generic AI phrasing and identical hashtag blocks.
- Cut on complete thoughts, normalize audio, and spot-check captions for Hinglish spelling errors.

## What you must know before building (the proactive list)

1. **Audio quality is the biggest risk.** A phone placed across the room gives poor speech recognition and unusable clips. Place it close, or use a cheap clip-on mic.
2. **Confidentiality filter.** You'll record work sessions. Client names, payments, screens showing credentials, and partner or family conversations must be blocked automatically, and a human should see every clip before it goes public, at least at first. A free review UI is a Telegram bot with approve/reject buttons. Bot uploads have a size cap (I recall 50 MB), so test your clip sizes.
3. **Retention rules conflict.** If you schedule clips over weeks, the finished clips must stay on disk until published (50 clips is roughly 1–2 GB). I'd keep each raw session until its review batch closes, or 72 hours, then delete it. One session at a time fits your 43 GB, but models (speech and LLM) will use 10–15 GB of it.
4. **Windows sleep.** The PC must stay awake during transfer and processing.
5. **Local LLM speed.** CPU-only candidate scoring over a 4-hour transcript is feasible but slow. Free cloud LLM tiers are an option, but check their data-use terms first, since this is private audio.
6. **Duplicate-post safety.** Record every publish attempt in SQLite with an idempotency key. After a timeout, check the platform before retrying.
